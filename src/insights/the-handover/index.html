The handover: an institution is finished only when it can run without its founder

Institutions are built to outlast the people who start them. Designing for succession from the first day is harder, and more important, than it sounds.

Every founder faces the same temptation. In the early days, it is faster to decide everything personally, to hold the relationships, to keep the important knowledge in one's head. The institution grows around that person like a vine around a post. Then, one day, the post is removed, by retirement, appointment elsewhere or simple exhaustion, and the vine falls over.

The pattern is common in public programmes launched by a single minister, in universities shaped by a long-serving vice-chancellor, in companies built by a founder who never quite let go. The work was real. The results were real. But the institution was never finished, because it could not stand without its builder.

Designing to be replaced

The alternative is to design the institution, from the beginning, to be handed over. That means writing down what is usually left unwritten: how decisions are made, by whom, under what rules, and how those rules can be changed. It means building governance that can say no to the founder. It means training successors early and giving them real authority to match their titles.

An institution is finished only when it can carry on without the person who built it.

It also means accepting a loss of control, which is the part most founders resist. An institution that can outlive its builder is, by definition, one its builder cannot fully command. The trade is worth making. Influence that rests on rules and relationships lasts longer than influence that rests on presence.

A test case

Aldier faced this question directly as Founding Partner to GENIVY, an international guild of students. From the outset, the firm committed in writing to hand every function, from finance to communications, to the students who run the guild. Its role was designed to shrink as theirs grows. That made some early decisions slower. It also meant that the institution's legitimacy rested with its members from the start.

Questions for builders

Anyone building an institution can test its readiness for handover with a few questions. If you left tomorrow, who would decide what you decide today? Are the rules that govern the institution written, public and changeable by someone other than you? Who among the next generation has already exercised real authority, and been allowed to get things wrong? What would your successor need to know that only you know now?

The answers are uncomfortable for most founders. That is precisely why they are worth asking early, while there is still time to act on them.
